You fixed a nasty bug at 2 a.m. The fix took three hours to find and about four lines to write. Somewhere out there, another developer is about to lose the same three hours.

That is the whole case for blogging as a developer. A good post saves strangers time, builds your reputation, and gives recruiters, clients, and open source maintainers something real to look at before they ever talk to you.

The hard part is not writing. It is picking where to publish. Hashnode, DEV, Medium, Bear Blog, Ghost, Hugo on GitHub Pages: every option has fans, and every fan will tell you the others are wrong.

So we sorted through them. This guide covers 23 **blogging sites for developers**, grouped by how they work, with honest notes on what each one is good at and where it will annoy you.

<!--more-->

## Quick Answer: What Is the Best Blogging Site for Developers?

**For most developers, the best starting point is Hashnode or DEV.** Both are free, both handle Markdown and code blocks well, and both put your writing in front of a built-in audience of programmers. Hashnode adds a custom domain and a GitHub backup. DEV has the bigger community.

**If you want full ownership and long-term SEO value, use a static site generator like [Hugo](https://gohugo.io) or [Astro](https://astro.build) on [GitHub Pages](https://pages.github.com)**, then cross-post to DEV or Hashnode with a canonical URL pointing back to your own site.

## How We Chose These 23 Platforms

We did not rank by popularity alone. Each platform was judged on the things developers actually care about:

- **Code-friendly writing:** Markdown support, syntax highlighting, and sane code block behavior
- **Ownership and portability:** custom domain support, easy export, and no lock-in
- **Search visibility:** clean HTML, fast load times, canonical URL control, and sitemap and RSS support
- **Audience:** whether readers show up on day one or you have to bring them
- **Cost:** what is free, what is paid, and what is quietly locked behind an upgrade
- **Maintenance:** how much time you will spend on the platform instead of on writing

Pricing and features change often, so treat the cost notes as a starting point and check each official site before you commit. You can read how we handle accuracy on our [editorial policy page](/editorial-policy/).

## Developer Communities and Publishing Networks

These platforms give you an audience on day one. The trade-off is that you are building on someone else's land.

### 1. DEV (Dev.to)

[DEV](https://dev.to) is built on the open source Forem software and is one of the friendliest places for programmers to share ideas. Posts are written in Markdown, tags make discovery easy, and the comment culture is more supportive than most corners of the internet.

**Why developers like it:** Free, fast to publish, supports a canonical URL in the front matter, and has series, reactions, and bookmarking built in.

**Watch out for:** No custom domain for your own posts, so your SEO benefit goes to DEV rather than to you.

**Best for:** Beginners, first posts, and getting early feedback.

### 2. Hashnode

[Hashnode](https://hashnode.com) is a blogging platform made for developers. You get a blog on your own custom domain, a Markdown editor, newsletters, series, and a GraphQL API if you want to go headless. It can also back up your posts to a GitHub repository, which is a big deal for portability.

**Why developers like it:** You keep your own domain and still get discovery through Hashnode's community feed.

**Watch out for:** Feed reach depends heavily on tags and early engagement.

**Best for:** Developers who want a real personal blog without managing infrastructure. If you are experimenting with AI automation, our walkthrough on [how to set up Hashnode for agents](/p/how-to-setup-hashnode-for-agents/) shows how to connect it to agent workflows.

### 3. Medium

[Medium](https://medium.com) still has one of the largest general readerships on the web, and its Partner Program can pay writers based on member reading time. It is great for opinion pieces, career stories, and explainers aimed at a broad tech audience.

**Why developers like it:** Polished reading experience and big audience reach.

**Watch out for:** Code formatting is basic (most people embed GitHub Gists), the paywall can limit reach, and you do not own the domain. Use Medium's story import tool so a canonical link points back to your original post.

**Best for:** Thought leadership and storytelling rather than code-heavy tutorials.

### 4. HackerNoon

[HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com) is a tech publication that accepts contributor stories through an editorial process. It leans toward startups, software engineering, AI, and crypto, and it can give your post a bigger megaphone than a brand new personal blog.

**Why developers like it:** Editorial review adds credibility, and the site has strong domain authority.

**Watch out for:** You submit and wait, so it is not instant publishing.

**Best for:** Opinion pieces, industry commentary, and launch stories.

### 5. freeCodeCamp News

[freeCodeCamp News](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/) is the publication side of the freeCodeCamp community. Writers apply to contribute and drafts go through editorial review. If you can explain a topic clearly for beginners, the audience here is huge.

**Why developers like it:** Massive reach for tutorials and a strong reputation for quality.

**Watch out for:** It is selective, and the house style leans toward step by step teaching.

**Best for:** Long, beginner-friendly tutorials.

### 6. Showwcase

[Showwcase](https://www.showwcase.com) is a developer network that blends portfolio profiles with posts and project showcases. It is a decent pick if you want your writing and your work history to live side by side.

**Best for:** Early-career developers building a visible profile.

### 7. Qiita and Zenn (Japan)

If you write in Japanese, [Qiita](https://qiita.com) and [Zenn](https://zenn.dev) are the two big developer communities in Japan. Both are Markdown friendly, and Zenn offers GitHub-based publishing. They are worth knowing about if your target readers are in Japan.

**Best for:** Japanese-language technical writing.

## Minimalist and Hosted Blogging Platforms

These give you a blog without a build pipeline. Less setup, more writing.

### 8. Bear Blog

[Bear Blog](https://bearblog.dev) is a minimalist, Markdown-powered blog and website builder. Pages are tiny, load almost instantly, and the editor stays out of your way. There is no tracking circus and no feed algorithm.

**Why developers like it:** Blazing fast, privacy friendly, and you can tweak the CSS freely.

**Watch out for:** Custom domains and some extras sit behind a small paid upgrade, so check the current plan. Discovery is mostly up to you.

**Best for:** Personal blogs, digital gardens, and writers who hate clutter.

### 9. JustBlogged

[JustBlogged](https://justblogged.com) is a newer hosted platform built on Cloudflare's edge infrastructure, aimed at getting a blog live in a couple of minutes. The free plan includes unlimited posts and pages plus custom domain support with SSL, while the Pro plan adds team collaboration, deeper theme customization, and removal of the JustBlogged branding.

**Why developers like it:** Fast global performance and a generous free tier.

**Watch out for:** It is a young product, so confirm export options and the roadmap before moving a large archive.

**Best for:** Developers who want a clean blog on their own domain without touching a build system. If you plan to point a domain at any hosted blog, the same DNS ideas from our [Blogger custom domain with Cloudflare guide](/p/blogger-custom-domain-cloudflare-setup/) apply.

### 10. Write.as

[Write.as](https://write.as) is a clean, distraction-free writing platform built on the open source WriteFreely project, which supports ActivityPub so your posts can be followed from the fediverse. It is a good fit for writers who care more about words than widgets.

**Best for:** Minimalist blogging and fediverse readers.

### 11. Micro.blog

[Micro.blog](https://micro.blog) is a paid, indie-friendly platform that supports Markdown, custom domains, cross-posting, and IndieWeb standards. It works well for short posts and regular notes rather than long tutorials.

**Best for:** Developers who like owning their feed and posting often.

### 12. Substack

[Substack](https://substack.com) is newsletter first. Every post can go to inboxes and the web at once, which is powerful if you want a loyal audience you can reach directly. It takes a cut of paid subscriptions, and code block support is basic.

**Best for:** Developer newsletters, opinion writing, and creator-led brands.

### 13. Ghost

[Ghost](https://ghost.org) is an open source publishing platform written in Node.js. It handles memberships, newsletters, and paid subscriptions natively, and you can self-host for free or pay for managed hosting. Syntax highlighting usually takes a small script or theme tweak.

**Why developers like it:** Modern, fast, SEO friendly out of the box, with real ownership.

**Watch out for:** Self-hosting means you own updates, backups, and email deliverability.

**Best for:** Developers building a paid or member-based publication.

### 14. WordPress

[WordPress](https://wordpress.org) powers a huge share of the web for a reason. The self-hosted version gives you thousands of plugins, a REST API, and the option to run it headless. A syntax highlighter plugin takes care of code blocks.

**Watch out for:** Security updates, plugin bloat, and hosting costs. A heavy theme can drag down Core Web Vitals.

**Best for:** Developers who want maximum flexibility, or who already know the ecosystem.

### 15. Blogger

[Blogger](https://www.blogger.com) is Google's free blogging platform. It supports custom domains, AdSense, and basic templates. It lacks the polish of newer tools, but it costs nothing and it is stable.

**Best for:** Absolute beginners and zero-budget experiments. Our [Blogger custom domain and Cloudflare setup](/p/blogger-custom-domain-cloudflare-setup/) tutorial walks through the DNS steps.

### 16. LinkedIn Articles

[LinkedIn Articles](https://www.linkedin.com) will not win any awards for code formatting, but it puts your writing in front of recruiters, hiring managers, and peers. It works best for career lessons, engineering leadership, and project retrospectives.

**Best for:** Professional visibility rather than deep technical tutorials.

### 17. Webflow

[Webflow](https://webflow.com) is a visual site builder with a built-in CMS and room for custom code embeds. It is a strong option for design-minded developers who want a polished portfolio with a blog attached. You will need a paid site plan to connect your own domain.

**Best for:** Portfolios where visual design matters as much as the writing.

## Static Site Generators and Self-Hosted Options

This is where you get full control, the best long-term SEO equity, and zero platform risk. The cost is setup time.

### 18. Hugo

[Hugo](https://gohugo.io) is a Go-based static site generator known for lightning-fast builds. You write in Markdown, pick a theme, and deploy to GitHub Pages, Netlify, or Cloudflare Pages. Even large sites build in seconds.

**Why developers like it:** Speed, simplicity, and a huge theme library.

**Watch out for:** Go templating has a learning curve.

**Best for:** Developers who want a fast, portable blog. Start with our [Hugo and GitHub Pages setup guide](/p/hugo-github-pages-setup/), then tune it with the [Hugo SEO guide](/p/hugo-seo-guide/).

### 19. Jekyll

[Jekyll](https://jekyllrb.com) is the Ruby-based generator that helped popularize blogging on GitHub Pages. It is mature, well documented, and a safe pick if you like Ruby or want a simple, battle-tested workflow.

**Best for:** Simple personal blogs and project sites.

### 20. Astro

[Astro](https://astro.build) is a modern framework that ships almost no JavaScript by default and supports Markdown and MDX through content collections. It is a great pick if you want a blog that can also grow into a full site with interactive components.

**Best for:** Developers who want modern tooling with excellent performance. See our guide to [deploying Astro on GitHub Pages](/p/how-to-deploy-astro-github-pages/).

### 21. Eleventy (11ty)

[Eleventy](https://www.11ty.dev) is a flexible, JavaScript-based generator that stays out of your way. It supports several template languages and has almost no opinions about structure, which some developers love and others find too open ended.

**Best for:** Minimalists who want total control.

### 22. Docusaurus

[Docusaurus](https://docusaurus.io) is a React-based framework from Meta that is built for documentation but includes a solid blog plugin. If you maintain an open source library or a developer tool, it lets you keep docs, changelogs, and blog posts in one place.

**Best for:** Product teams and open source maintainers. If you write docs, the [Diataxis documentation framework guide](/p/diataxis-documentation-framework-guide/) helps you structure tutorials, how-tos, references, and explanations.

### 23. GitHub Pages

[GitHub Pages](https://pages.github.com) is not a blog engine. It is free static hosting that pairs perfectly with Hugo, Jekyll, Astro, or Eleventy. You get HTTPS, custom domain support, and automated deploys through GitHub Actions.

**Best for:** Free, reliable hosting for any static blog. Our guide to a [GitHub Pages custom domain with Cloudflare](/p/github-pages-custom-domain-cloudflare/), our [Utterances comments setup](/p/what-is-utterances-setup-guide/), and [IndexNow setup](/p/setup-and-ping-indexnow-api-key/) cover the details. If you want a free subdomain to start, see our [free subdomains for developers list](/p/free-subdomains-for-developers-list/).

## Pro Tip: Own Your Domain, Syndicate Everywhere Else

The strongest long-term setup is boring and effective:

1. Publish the original post on **your own domain** (a Hugo, Astro, Ghost, or Hashnode custom domain blog).
2. Wait a few days so search engines can index the original.
3. Cross-post to DEV, Hashnode, or Medium and set the **canonical URL** to your original.

On DEV, that is a single line in the front matter:

```yaml
---
title: How I Fixed the Race Condition in Our Queue Worker
published: true
tags: javascript, nodejs, debugging
canonical_url: https://yourdomain.com/blog/fix-queue-race-condition
---
```

On your own site, make sure the original page declares itself in the HTML head:

```html
<link rel="canonical" href="https://yourdomain.com/blog/fix-queue-race-condition" />
```

Google's documentation on [consolidating duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls) explains how canonical signals work. Used correctly, cross-posting grows your audience without splitting your ranking.

## Actionable Tips for Better Developer Blog Posts

Good platform choice gets you about 20 percent of the way. The rest is how you write.

- **Solve one specific problem per post.** "Fix CORS errors in Express with credentials" beats "Everything about CORS."
- **Put the answer near the top.** Search engines and AI assistants both pull short, direct answers. Give the fix first, then explain.
- **Use real error messages in headings.** Developers search for exact strings. Naming them helps you rank.
- **Show working code.** Test every snippet, label the language in your code fences, and mention versions (Node 22, Python 3.13, Astro 5) so readers know if it still applies.
- **Use clear H2 and H3 headings.** They help skimmers, featured snippets, and AI search engines understand your structure. Our [Markdown syntax guide](/p/markdown-syntax-guide-for-dev9b/) is a handy reference.
- **Add Open Graph images and descriptions.** Posts with proper previews get far more clicks on social platforms.
- **Update old posts.** Refresh version numbers and add a visible "last updated" date. Fresh and accurate beats old and popular.
- **Ping search engines.** If you run your own site, set up [IndexNow](/p/setup-and-ping-indexnow-api-key/) so new posts get discovered faster.
- **Build an RSS feed.** Many developers still live in feed readers, and most static generators create one automatically.

## How to Get Cited by AI Search Engines

ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity all lean on clear, well-structured, trustworthy pages. A few habits make your posts easier for them to quote:

- **Lead with a direct answer** in the first paragraph, then add detail below it.
- **Use question-style subheadings** such as "How do I set up a custom domain?" and answer them in two or three sentences.
- **Add an FAQ section** with schema markup, as shown at the end of this article.
- **Show who wrote it.** A real author name, a short bio, and links to your GitHub or LinkedIn build trust.
- **Name tools and versions precisely.** Consistent entity names (Hugo, GitHub Pages, Cloudflare) help machines connect your content to the right topic.
- **Date everything.** Publish dates and update dates help engines judge freshness.

## Common Mistakes Developers Make When Choosing a Blog Platform

- **Starting with the most complex setup.** A perfect custom blog that never gets a post is worse than a messy DEV profile with twenty articles.
- **Never owning a domain.** Platforms change rules, pricing, and algorithms. A domain you control survives all of it.
- **Ignoring export options.** Before you commit, check that you can get your posts out as Markdown.
- **Cross-posting without canonical URLs.** This creates duplicate content and can muddy your rankings.
- **Chasing the platform instead of the readers.** Pick one place, publish consistently for three months, then reassess.

## Key Takeaways

- **Hashnode and DEV** are the best low-friction starting points for developers who want an audience quickly.
- **Hugo, Astro, or Jekyll on GitHub Pages** give you the most ownership, speed, and long-term SEO value.
- **Ghost and Substack** are the strongest choices if you want a newsletter or paid membership.
- **Bear Blog, JustBlogged, and Write.as** are great when you want a fast, simple blog with minimal setup.
- **Medium, HackerNoon, and freeCodeCamp News** are best for reach, not ownership.
- **Own a domain** and treat third-party platforms as distribution channels.
- **Use canonical URLs** whenever you cross-post.
- **Write answer-first posts** with clear headings and tested code so both Google and AI search engines can quote you.
- **Consistency beats platform choice.** Ship a post every week or two for a few months and the compounding starts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best blogging site for developers?

Hashnode and DEV are the best blogging sites for most developers because they are free, support Markdown and code blocks, and come with built-in developer audiences. For full ownership and SEO control, a static site generator like Hugo or Astro hosted on GitHub Pages is the stronger long-term choice.

### What is the best free blogging platform for programmers?

DEV, Hashnode, Blogger, and GitHub Pages are all free to start. DEV and Hashnode offer the easiest setup and an existing audience. GitHub Pages with Hugo, Jekyll, or Astro costs nothing and gives you full control, but it takes more setup time.

### Is Hashnode better than DEV?

Hashnode is better if you want a blog on your own custom domain with GitHub backup and a headless API. DEV is better if you want a large, welcoming community and simple publishing. Many developers use both and cross-post with a canonical URL.

### Should developers blog on Medium?

Medium works well for opinion pieces, career stories, and general tech topics with broad appeal. It is weaker for code-heavy tutorials because of basic code formatting and no custom domain. Import your posts from your own site so the canonical link protects your SEO.

### Can I use my own domain with a developer blog?

Yes. Hashnode, Ghost, WordPress, Webflow, Bear Blog, JustBlogged, Blogger, and any static site on GitHub Pages all support custom domains, though some features or plans may require payment. DEV and Medium generally do not let you host your personal posts on your own domain.

### How do I avoid duplicate content when cross-posting?

Publish on your own site first, wait a few days, then cross-post and set the canonical URL to your original post. DEV supports a canonical_url field in front matter, and Medium's import tool adds a canonical link automatically.

### Is a static site generator better than a hosted platform?

A static site generator gives you speed, ownership, and portable Markdown files, which is excellent for long-term SEO. A hosted platform is better if you want to publish in minutes and tap into an existing audience. Many developers combine both.

## Conclusion

Picking a platform comes down to your priorities. If you want an audience today, start with DEV or Hashnode. If you want compounding SEO and full ownership, build with Hugo or Astro on GitHub Pages. Either way, the most important thing is to publish consistently.
